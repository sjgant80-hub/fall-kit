Phase 4 of the estate going live · ten claims sealed before the code

An organism that sees what it is bad at, and grows what it lacks

A mesh of the foundation's nodes does real work: five kinds of question, asked of real public data. A self-model watches every answer against what turned out to be true. It finds the questions the mesh answers badly or not at all, and has a new specialist grown for the worst of them. That specialist is let in only if it proves itself on data it never saw. Then the organism carries on, and does it again, with no one turning the crank, until a limit sealed in advance.

Every step leaves a receipt: what was seen, which gap was chosen, what was grown, the numbers it was judged on, and whether it got in. This is bounded improvement you can check, not open-ended self-improvement, and the claims say so.

    How it works

    1. Demand first. It grows only for questions the mesh is actually asked and answers badly: the self-model ranks gaps by the asks they lose. A specialist abstains, rather than guess, on an input that lacks a column it reads.
    2. Planted. Two askers, a decent shop holder, and a weak bank holder (the growth engine's starting point, never evolved, on three columns). Three of the five kinds of question have nobody to answer them.
    3. A round of work. 300 real rows of each kind of question, asked through the mesh as signed packets. Each holder's door checks every ask; each asker's door checks every answer and its receipt.
    4. The self-model looks. It joins what was asked, what came back and what turned out true, and counts by question, by answering node and by link. It ranks the gaps by what they cost: asks × (1 − balanced accuracy). It flags any link the mesh trusts whose answers are often wrong. It predicts every score for the next round.
    5. It grows for the worst gap. First a control: a node grown on the same rows with their answers scrambled, which should be useless. Then the real candidate. Each is grown on FIT rows only, keeping the best of the dataset's column groups.
    6. The gate. On GATE rows it never saw, a node must be better than guessing (the lower edge of a 95% bootstrap range above 55%), and better than the organism without it by at least 2 points, with the lower edge of that gain above zero. If it passes, the founder signs it onto the roster and drops a holder it beat.
    7. Bounded. At most one join a cycle, each gap tried once, at most four nodes grown, at most eight rounds. It stops by one of its rules and keeps working.

    The failures, read plainly

    G2, the control that got in. The control for the census question was grown on scrambled answers, and the gate still let it in: it scored about 69% on rows it never saw. The other controls sat near guessing, so the scrambling works. The reason is the growth engine itself. It starts every search from one genome, "more of each column counts towards yes", and for income in a census that is already true of age, education and hours. With scrambled answers to learn from, the search finds nothing better and keeps its start. So a node grown on noise is not blank here, and the gate was right that it beats guessing. The sealed claim said the control would be refused every time. It was not, and the claim is kept as failed. It also means the census gap counted in A2 was filled by that control node, not by a grown candidate: it was admitted by the gate and does answer at 69%, so A2 counts it, and says which it was.

    G3, the node that was good on average. The bank node joined with 72% on rows it never saw, and over all its work rows it scores the same. But the bank's calls are in date order, and the organism's eight rounds of work were the campaign's first months, before the crash, when almost nobody subscribed. On those, the node's pattern (read from the economy's own numbers) says no to everyone, which scores exactly 50%, the same as the weak holder it replaced. It did no harm, but it did not help where it worked, and the claim is kept as failed. The order of the rows was never tuned: it is the order the data is kept in.

    What they mean. A gate is only as good as the slice it judges on. A held-out sample shows how a node does on average; work that arrives in time order can sit in a corner of that average. And a control is only a null if the engine growing it starts from nothing. Both are fixes for a next phase, measured as their own claims: judge on the newest rows, and start a control's search from a blank genome.

    For a business

    What it gives you: a system that notices which questions it answers badly, builds and checks its own specialists for them, and shows you the evidence for every one it lets in, or keeps out.

      Security and data protection, answered straight

      • What it stops. A specialist that does not beat guessing, or does not beat what you already have, on data it never saw. Growth past its sealed limits. A member the founder's roster does not name.
      • What it does not stop. A specialist that is good on average and bad on the work it actually gets: G3 shows exactly that. A dataset that is itself wrong or biased: the gate measures accuracy against the data's own answers, nothing more.
      • Where data goes. Nowhere: growth, gating and the mesh all run on your machines, and this page runs in your tab.
      • Personal data. The specialists are a few readable rules over columns you choose to give them, so what each one uses is visible. Give it only the columns you may use for that purpose (UK GDPR and GDPR, purpose limitation and minimisation), and keep a person on any decision with legal effect on someone.

      Rolling it out

      1. Start with questions you already know the answers to later: will this customer renew, will this delivery be late. The self-model needs the truth to arrive, eventually.
      2. Seal the limits first: how many specialists, how often, what score counts as good. Then let it run.
      3. Read the receipts weekly, the refusals especially. Promote what helps; drop what does not.

      Risks, answered

      • "It is just retraining." No retraining is automatic here: a specialist is grown only for a gap the self-model found, judged on data it never saw, and let in only under sealed limits, with a receipt either way.
      • "The data is old and public." Yes, on purpose: anyone can check the numbers. Your own data plugs in the same way: rows, a truth that arrives, and the three-way split.
      • "Some claims failed." Yes, and they stay on the receipt with the reasons. A system that only ever reports passes is the one to worry about.
      • "Scorecards are simple." They are, deliberately: readable, small, and cheap to check. Where a question needs more, the gate will say so by refusing.

      The case for the board

      The organisation gets capability that grows where the work shows it is missing, with evidence attached to every addition and every refusal, inside limits set in advance, and re-runnable by anyone. That is the difference between automation you have to trust and automation you can audit.

      The paper: bounded growth you can check

      The problem. A system that can add to itself is only useful if you can tell when an addition helps. Most retraining loops judge a new model on data that leaked from its training, accept it on faith, and keep no record of what they turned down.

      The design. Separate seeing from growing from admitting. A self-model sees the organism against the truth: every question, every answering node, every link. It does not change anything; it ranks the gaps by cost. A growth engine grows a candidate for the worst gap from one slice of data. A gate admits it only on a second slice it never saw, against chance and against the organism without it, with the uncertainty measured. The organism's real work is a third slice nobody touched. Limits are sealed before the first run, and a control grown on scrambled answers is offered first, to show the gate can say no.

      What is measured. Ten claims were committed before any of Phase 4's code, then re-sealed once, before any code, to close a hole in the gate (the reason is in the claims file). The self-model is checked count for count against a separate tally; its gap lists and flags against their definitions; its predictions against what happened. Every candidate and control is checked against the gate's own rule. The grown organism is compared with the planted one, ask by ask, on the same work. The whole life is replayed by CI to the same bytes.

      Honest limits. The specialists are interpretable scorecards, not neural networks. The data is public and historical. The founder's key sits with the growth policy, inside its sealed limits. Bounded improvement with receipts is all that is claimed: not open-ended self-improvement, and not awareness.

      Questions

      Is this a self-improving AI?

      It is bounded self-improvement with receipts, and no more. The organism watches its own work, finds where it is weakest, grows a specialist for that one gap and lets it in only if it proves itself on data it never saw. It stops at limits sealed in advance. It does not rewrite itself, it does not grow without limit, and nothing here claims awareness.

      What does the self-model see that the mesh cannot?

      Whether answers were right. A mesh trusts a node more each time its receipt checks, but a receipt proves who answered and what, not that the answer was true. The self-model joins what was asked, what came back and what the world later revealed, so it sees a node the mesh trusts completely and that is often wrong, and flags it.

      How does a new node earn its place?

      It is grown only on one slice of the data, then judged on a second slice it never saw. It joins only if, on that slice, it is clearly better than guessing and clearly better than what the organism already had, with the uncertainty measured. The work it then does is a third slice, used by nobody before.

      What did not hold?

      Read the receipt on the page: every failed claim stays on it, with the reason. Two kinds of failure are explained in full there: a control grown on scrambled answers that still carried real signal, and a node that was good on average but whose actual work came from a period it did badly on.

      Where does the data come from?

      Five public datasets from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, each under CC BY 4.0: online shoppers, bank telemarketing, the 1994 US census extract, Taiwanese credit card clients and in-vehicle coupon offers. They are read through gated loaders at a pinned commit, and every file is checked against its recorded SHA-256 before anything runs.
