Phase 7 of the estate going live · thirteen claims sealed before the code

Your system, as a living node, or as your own organism

We take a business's own software and make it a node: a program with its own key that checks every message before it reads it, keeps the evidence of what it did, and learns from every attack it sees: a message it refuses once is refused on sight after that, and a neighbour's warning is taken only after the node has checked it for itself. Then the business chooses how it lives.

Model A, an organ of a larger mesh. The node joins the estate's mesh of nodes. It answers the others, the others check its answers, and an attack one of them sees is refused on sight by all of them.

Model B, an organism of its own. The same software grows into the business's own mesh, from a seed phrase only the business holds: its own keys, its own nodes, its own records, on its own machines. Nothing of ours is in it. Coupling to a larger mesh is the business's choice, and shares only what it says.

Here the client build is a real booking engine: the one built for an off-grid glamping site, run on its own demo calendar. Its guests never leave its node. The site's live system was not touched, and it has not joined anything: the mesh it joins here is made from public test keys.

    How it works

    1. The client's software becomes a node. Its own gated code stays its own; around it goes the estate's shared foundation, copied in byte for byte: a key, a door that checks every message before reading a byte of it, an immune memory, a record of what it used and what it gave.
    2. It answers without giving its customers away. Asked whether a cabin is free, it answers yes or no with a receipt that names the question, the answer and a digest of what its calendar says about occupancy: never a guest, a booking or a figure. The client's own audit, which holds the calendar, can re-run every answer: it is one function in the client's own node (auditStay), given the calendar and an answer, and it says whether the answer is the calendar's.
    3. It checks what it can be asked to check. Asked to guard against double bookings, it merges the feeds it is sent with its own engine and names every clash. The node that asked keeps what it sent and runs the engine again on it.
    4. Model A: it joins a mesh. Who joins is the founder's decision: the mesh's founder signs a roster of its members, a node joins when its card is on it, and it leaves when the founder signs a roster without it. Each node pins its peers from that roster and from nothing else. An attack refused anywhere is refused on sight by every node that takes the antibody, and only after each has re-run it on its own door. Every answer used is recorded, under the node's own name.
    5. Model B: it grows its own. A seed phrase only the client holds works out every key of the client's organism: its founder, its roster, its genesis, its nodes. The estate's packets are refused at its doors because it pins none of the estate's keys. If one of its booking engines dies, the desk asks the other.
    6. Coupled, by choice. A client who wants to can plant on a genesis the estate and the client name together, and let its desk pin the estate's immune hub. Its records then merge with the estate's, and the hub's antibodies reach its desk.
    7. The wedge. The liveware pilot, sealed and measured before this phase, re-runs; and its gateway, the same code on the client's own key table, becomes the client's front door.

    Security and data protection, answered straight

    • What it stops. Messages from keys not on the founder's roster; forged and changed messages; replays; a node taking another's name; an antibody that would make a node refuse its own genuine traffic; a merge into a network that does not bind names; a live allocation without the switch.
    • What it does not stop. A stolen key or seed phrase (it is the owner's word); a compromised machine; a member on the roster that answers wrongly about data only it holds (the client's own audit catches that, the mesh cannot).
    • Where data goes. The client's calendar stays on its node. Answers to the mesh carry the question, the verdict and a digest of occupancy, no names, ids or figures. In Model B nothing reaches the estate at all.
    • The demo keys and phrase are public. Everything here is grown from published test seeds, so anyone can make messages these nodes accept. A real deployment uses keys and a phrase its owner holds.

    1. Start with the wedge. Run the pilot on one real feed of yours, sealed before it runs, and read its receipts by role.

    4. Model B, step by step. Choose a long phrase that only you hold and keep it as you would a key (the box on this page is for trying, not for a real one). The seed works out your founder's key and one key for each node you name; each node starts on your machine with its key; your founder signs the roster of your nodes; each node pins the others from it. Lose the phrase and you lose the keys; share it and you share them.
    5. If you couple, couple on purpose. Name a genesis together, pin the hub from every node that should learn from it, and keep your roster your own.
    6. Keep the switch off until your board decides, with advice, what any allocation would mean.

    The paper: client builds as organs and organisms

    The problem. A small business runs on software that holds its customers and answers its questions, usually rented, trusted on the provider's word, and alone when it is attacked.

    The design. Make the business's own software a node of one shared foundation: a key, a door that checks before it reads, an immune memory, and a record of work. Let it join a wider mesh that answers for it and shares immunity (Model A), or grow into the business's own organism from a seed only the business holds (Model B), coupled to a wider mesh only by choice. Prove it small first, with a pilot whose claims are sealed before it runs.

    What is measured. Thirteen claims were committed before any of Phase 7's code: that the client's repository carries the foundation byte for byte; that its node joins from the roster, answers, checks, keeps its guests, shares immunity both ways and is recorded; that the seed grows the client's own organism, sovereign, surviving a node's loss; that coupling shares only what it says; that the pilot re-runs and its gateway is the client's front door; that a browser grows the same organism; and that nothing barred and none of the guests is published.

    Honest limits. The client build runs on its demo calendar and public test keys. Immunity across a coupled organism's edge stops at the bridge (B3, failed and kept). A seed phrase is as strong as the phrase.

    Words used here

    • Node: a program with its own key that checks every message at its door before reading it, keeps an immune memory and records its work.
    • Organ, organism: a node in a larger mesh (Model A); a business's own mesh of nodes, grown from its own seed (Model B).
    • Seed phrase: the one secret every key of an organism is worked out from. As strong as the phrase; kept like a key.
    • Roster: the founder's signed list of the members. A node pins its peers from it and from nothing else.
    • Genesis: the first entry of the records: who may switch them on and who may measure work, fixed before anything is recorded.
    • Antibody: what a node keeps of a message it refused, so the same message is refused on sight; adopted from a peer only after re-running it on its own door.
    • Coupled: a client's organism planted on a genesis it and a larger mesh name together, with its desk pinning the mesh's immune hub. The client's choice.
    • Wedge: the small, real, sealed pilot a business checks first.

    Questions

    Does the client's data leave its node?

    No. Asked whether a cabin is free, the node answers with the cabin, the dates, yes or no, and a digest of what its calendar says about occupancy. Every byte it sent in the run was read, raw and decoded, for the guests' names, the bookings' ids, their figures, and anything shaped like an email address or a phone number, and none was found.

    What is the difference between Model A and Model B?

    In Model A the client's node joins the estate's mesh: the estate's founder puts it on the roster, it answers the other nodes and shares their immunity, and its work is recorded in the estate's records. In Model B the same software grows into the client's own mesh from a seed phrase only the client holds: none of the estate's keys is in it, and the estate's messages are refused at its doors.

    Can a Model B organism ever join the larger mesh?

    Yes, by the client's choice: planted on a genesis the client and the estate name together, its records merge with the estate's with nothing refused, and its desk can take the estate's antibodies. Today those antibodies stop at the desk; the client's other nodes need to pin the hub too.

    What did not hold?

    One claim of thirteen: coupled, the client's desk adopted the estate hub's antibodies but had nothing to pass on, because a node shares on only what it refused itself. It is kept as failed, with the reason and what to do about it.
